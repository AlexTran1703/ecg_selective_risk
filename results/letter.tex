Reader disagreement during Electrocardiography (ECG) interpretation may signal 
cases near diagnostic boundaries, yet conventional evaluation typically reduces the adjudication
process to a single final reference label, discarding information about how
readily consensus was reached. Automated ECG interpretation has shown strong
performance in rhythm and conduction diagnosis, particularly for arrhythmias,
and has increasingly been extended to derive clinically relevant information
beyond categorical classification.\cite{ribeiro2020,wei2022} Prior work has
demonstrated limited expert agreement in difficult AI--reader discordant ECGs,
while uncertainty estimation can support referral of lower-confidence automated
interpretations for expert review.\cite{vranken2021,mayourian2025} We therefore
examined whether uncertainty-based referral reduces the burden of automated
diagnostic error and whether reader disagreement identifies ECGs with
persistently elevated patient-level error risk across retained coverage.

This retrospective secondary analysis used the Clinical Outcomes in Digital
Electrocardiology (CODE) datasets from the Telehealth Network of Minas Gerais,
Brazil, comprising standard 12-lead ECGs acquired predominantly with TEB ECGPC
or ErgoPC 13 devices.\cite{ribeiro2020} CODE-15\%, including 345,779 ECGs from
233,770 patients recorded between 2010 and 2016, served as the model-development
cohort.\cite{code15} Independent evaluation used CODE-test, an
arrhythmia- and conduction-focused set of 827 ECGs from 827 patients collected
in 2018. Each ECG was independently interpreted by 2 cardiologists for 6
prespecified rhythm and conduction diagnoses, with disagreements adjudicated by
a third senior specialist.\cite{ribeiro2020} The analysis used only publicly
available, de-identified data; the source CODE study was approved by the
Research Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais
(protocol 49368496317.7.0000.5149).\cite{ribeiro2020}

An ensemble of 5 lightweight 1-dimensional convolutional neural networks with
the same architecture was trained using different random seeds, and their
predicted probabilities were averaged. Following prior work, model confidence
was defined from the diagnosis-specific decision threshold, and whole ECGs were
referred according to their least confident diagnosis.\cite{vranken2021}

For each ECG, confidence was defined by its least confident diagnosis, and
progressively lower-confidence ECGs were referred according to the previously
described uncertainty framework.\cite{vranken2021} Error burden was defined as
the proportion of retained ECGs containing at least 1 incorrect diagnosis among
the 6 labels. Reader disagreement was then examined in relation to this error
burden. The primary comparison used a risk ratio (RR) to compare the occurrence
of any model error between ECGs with reader disagreement and those with reader
consensus, while a prevalence ratio (PR) descriptively quantified enrichment of
reader disagreement among ECGs containing any model error relative to the
overall cohort. Confidence intervals were estimated by bootstrap resampling of
whole ECGs, preserving all 6 labels within each ECG.

Diagnostic errors were concentrated in the lowest-confidence ECGs. At full
coverage, 61 of 827 ECGs (7.4\%) contained at least 1 diagnostic error,
decreasing to 40 ECGs (5.1\%) at 95\% coverage and 29 ECGs (3.9\%) at
90\% coverage (Figure 1A). This progressive reduction in error burden as
lower-confidence ECGs were referred indicates that confidence distinguished
more from less reliable automated ECG interpretations.

Across 80\% to 100\% coverage, ECGs with reader disagreement maintained a
consistently higher patient-level diagnostic error rate than those with reader
consensus (Figure 1B), rather than the excess risk emerging only as
lower-confidence ECGs were added.  The mean error-risk difference across this range was
26.4 percentage points (95\% CI: 6.4--49.7).

At full coverage, any diagnostic error occurred in 11 of 33 ECGs with reader
disagreement (33.3\%) compared with 50 of 794 ECGs with reader consensus
(6.3\%; RR: 5.29; 95\% CI: 2.70--8.85). This full-coverage association was
significant by two-sided Fisher exact test ($P<0.001$). Reader disagreement
was uncommon overall, occurring in 33 of 827 ECGs (4.0\%), but was present in
11 of 61 ECGs containing any diagnostic error (18.0\%; PR: 4.5; 95\% CI:
2.6--6.8). Thus, reader disagreement was enriched among error-containing ECGs
and marked a subgroup with persistently greater diagnostic error risk across
the examined referral range.

In 9 of the 11 ECGs with both reader disagreement and diagnostic error, the
error involved the disputed diagnosis; in the remaining 2, it involved a
different diagnosis. Thus, disagreement most often marked difficulty in the
same diagnostic decision, while occasionally reflecting broader interpretive
difficulty. Disagreement should not be interpreted as evidence that the
adjudicated reference was incorrect, but rather as a marker of an interpretive
boundary where independent cardiologists did not initially converge, consistent
with prior expert readjudication of difficult AI--reader discordant
ECGs.\cite{mayourian2025}

Uncertainty-based referral reduced overall error burden but did not eliminate
the excess risk associated with reader disagreement. Automated diagnostic
uncertainty supports referral, whereas reader disagreement marks residual
diagnostic difficulty that persists after lower-confidence ECGs are excluded.
In arrhythmia assessment, these findings suggest that a single inconclusive
tracing may prompt repeat or longitudinal evaluation. More broadly, collapsing
expert annotations into one adjudicated label discards information about label
reliability; preserving individual reader labels and adjudication history may
therefore improve evaluation of automated ECG systems. This retrospective analysis 
was based on a modest number of disagreement ECGs;
therefore, the findings warrant confirmation in larger cohorts.

\noindent\textbf{Figure 1. Uncertainty-Based Referral and Reader Disagreement in Automated ECG Diagnosis.}
\textbf{(A)} Uncertainty-based referral evaluates whether lower-confidence
whole ECGs concentrate automated diagnostic error, using the least confident
diagnosis to determine ECG-level confidence. Shading represents 95\% ECG-level
bootstrap confidence intervals.
\textbf{(B)} Reader-agreement stratification evaluates whether reader
disagreement identifies residual diagnostic risk across retained coverage after
the same confidence-based referral procedure. The corresponding risk ratio
(RR) compares patient-level diagnostic error between ECGs with reader
disagreement and reader consensus; the dashed line denotes RR = 1.
Circles mark the annotated coverage levels and shaded regions represent
95\% ECG-level bootstrap confidence intervals.
ECG = electrocardiogram; RR = risk ratio.
