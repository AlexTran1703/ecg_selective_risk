\begin{abstract}
Reader disagreement may preserve diagnostic difficulty lost after adjudication
to a single reference label. We evaluated uncertainty-based referral and reader
disagreement in CODE-test (827 12-lead ECGs). Overall diagnostic error decreased
from 7.4\% at full coverage to 3.9\% at 90\% coverage, but
disagreement-associated excess error persisted. At full coverage, error occurred
in 33.3\% of disagreement ECGs versus 6.3\% with consensus (RR, 5.29; 95\% CI,
2.70--8.85). From 90\% to 100\% coverage, RR and PR remained above 1 with 95\%
CIs excluding 1, indicating residual diagnostic difficulty despite
confidence-based referral.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reader disagreement during electrocardiographic (ECG) interpretation may signal
cases near diagnostic boundaries, yet conventional evaluation typically reduces
the adjudication process to a single final reference label, discarding
information about how readily consensus was reached. Automated ECG
interpretation has shown strong performance in rhythm and conduction diagnosis,
particularly for arrhythmias, and has increasingly been extended to derive
clinically relevant information beyond categorical classification \cite{ribeiro2020,wei2022}.
Prior work has demonstrated limited expert agreement in difficult AI--reader
discordant ECGs, while uncertainty estimation can support referral of
lower-confidence automated interpretations for expert review \cite{vranken2021,mayourian2025}.
We therefore evaluated whether uncertainty-based referral reduced overall
automated diagnostic error and whether excess patient-level error risk associated
with reader disagreement declined in parallel or persisted across retained
coverage.

\section{Materials and methods}

This retrospective secondary analysis used the Clinical Outcomes in Digital
Electrocardiology (CODE) datasets from the Telehealth Network of Minas Gerais,
Brazil, comprising standard 12-lead ECGs acquired predominantly with TEB ECGPC
or ErgoPC 13 devices \cite{ribeiro2020}. CODE-15\%, containing 345,779 ECGs
from 233,770 patients recorded between 2010 and 2016, served as the
model-development cohort \cite{code15}. Independent evaluation used CODE-test,
an arrhythmia- and conduction-focused set of 827 ECGs from 827 patients
collected in 2018. Each ECG was independently interpreted by 2 cardiologists
for 6 prespecified rhythm and conduction diagnoses, with disagreements
adjudicated by a third senior specialist \cite{ribeiro2020}.

An ensemble of 5 lightweight 1-dimensional convolutional neural networks with
the same architecture was trained using different random seeds, and predicted
probabilities were averaged. Following prior work, diagnosis-specific confidence
was defined relative to the decision threshold, and each ECG was assigned the
confidence of its least confident diagnosis \cite{vranken2021}. Progressively
lower-confidence ECGs were referred from automated interpretation.

Error burden was the proportion of retained ECGs with at least 1 incorrect
diagnosis among the 6 labels. At each coverage level, RR compared error risk
between ECGs with reader disagreement and consensus, while PR quantified the
enrichment of reader disagreement among error-containing ECGs relative to all
retained ECGs. Confidence intervals were obtained from 1000 whole-ECG bootstrap
resamples using the percentile method, with ratios recalculated within each
replicate.

Diagnostic errors were concentrated in the lowest-confidence ECGs. At full
coverage, 61 of 827 ECGs (7.4\%) contained at least 1 diagnostic error,
decreasing to 40 ECGs (5.1\%) at 95\% coverage and 29 ECGs (3.9\%) at 90\%
coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:referral}). Thus, referral of lower-confidence
ECGs progressively reduced
the retained error burden.

Across 80\% to 100\% coverage, ECGs with reader disagreement maintained a
consistently higher patient-level diagnostic error rate than those with reader
consensus (Fig.~\ref{fig:disagreement}A), rather than the excess risk
emerging only as lower-confidence ECGs were added. 

Given that only 33 ECGs had reader disagreement, ratio estimates became
increasingly imprecise at lower retained coverage, as reflected by the widening
confidence intervals. From 90\% through 100\% coverage, however, both the RR
and PR remained above the null value of 1, with their 95\% confidence intervals
also excluding 1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:disagreement}B). Thus, reader disagreement
remained associated with greater diagnostic error risk and was consistently
enriched among error-containing ECGs even after lower-confidence ECGs were
referred.

At full coverage, any diagnostic error occurred in 11 of 33 ECGs with reader
disagreement (33.3\%) compared with 50 of 794 ECGs with reader consensus
(6.3\%; RR, 5.29; 95\% CI, 2.70--8.85; two-sided Fisher exact $P<0.001$).
Reader disagreement occurred in 33 of 827 ECGs (4.0\%) overall but in 11 of 61
ECGs containing any diagnostic error (18.0\%; PR, 4.5; 95\% CI, 2.6--6.8).
In 9 of the 11 ECGs with both reader disagreement and diagnostic error, the error
involved the disputed diagnosis; in the remaining 2, it involved a different
diagnosis.

\section{Discussion}

Uncertainty-based referral reduced overall error burden but did not eliminate the
excess risk associated with reader disagreement. Disagreement therefore marked a
subgroup with residual diagnostic difficulty even after lower-confidence ECGs
were excluded. It should not be interpreted as evidence that the adjudicated
reference was incorrect, but rather as a marker of an interpretive boundary where
independent cardiologists did not initially converge, consistent with prior expert
readjudication of difficult AI--reader discordant ECGs \cite{mayourian2025}.

These findings distinguish two complementary signals in automated ECG evaluation:
model confidence can identify interpretations suitable for referral, whereas
reader disagreement preserves information about reference-label reliability that
is lost when adjudication is collapsed to a single final label. Preserving
individual reader labels and adjudication history may therefore improve evaluation
of automated ECG systems. This retrospective analysis included a modest number of
disagreement ECGs, and the findings warrant confirmation in larger cohorts.
