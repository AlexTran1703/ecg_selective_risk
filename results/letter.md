Reader Disagreement Identifies Electrocardiographic Labels at Increased Risk of Automated Diagnostic Error

Artificial intelligence (AI) achieves high diagnostic performance on the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), but that performance is judged against an expert-derived reference.(1) Uncertainty estimation can flag difficult predictions for expert referral, and model certainty is lower when cardiologists disagree.(3) Readjudication studies show that apparent discordance between AI and an original reader often arises in genuinely difficult interpretations.(4) Yet reference construction ends with a single adjudicated diagnosis, and the disagreement preceding that label is discarded. We examined whether cardiologist disagreement on an individual ECG diagnosis identifies labels at increased risk of automated diagnostic error, and whether uncertainty-based referral preferentially removes the error-prone tail.

This retrospective secondary analysis used the public CODE datasets. CODE-15%, containing 345,779 examinations from 233,770 patients, was used exclusively for model development.(1,2) Five conventional lightweight 1-dimensional convolutional networks, differing only in random seed, were trained on one frozen patient-level training-validation partition and their probabilities averaged. Class-specific decision thresholds for first-degree atrioventricular block, right and left bundle branch block, sinus bradycardia, atrial fibrillation, and sinus tachycardia were fixed using validation data alone. The independent CODE-test set comprised 827 unique-patient ECGs, each interpreted independently by 2 cardiologists, with disagreement resolved by a third senior specialist. Reader disagreement was defined at the diagnosis-record level and entered no training, threshold, or referral decision. Model confidence was the distance from the diagnosis-specific operating threshold. Selective risk was computed after progressively referring lower-confidence predictions. Confidence intervals were obtained by resampling whole ECGs, preserving dependence among the 6 labels from each tracing. The disagreement-error association was additionally estimated using a modified-Poisson generalized estimating equation with robust standard errors clustered by ECG and adjusted for diagnosis. The ensemble achieved a macro area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve of 0.989 and a macro area under the precision-recall curve of 0.801. Errors concentrated among lower-confidence predictions: macro selective risk decreased from 0.0125 (95% CI: 0.0095-0.0157) at full coverage to 0.0013 (95% CI: 0.0004-0.0034) at 90% coverage and 0.0003 (95% CI: 0.0000-0.0010) at 80% coverage (Figure 1A). Residual errors decreased from 62 at full coverage to 18 at 95% and 6 at 90%.

Reader disagreement was uncommon, occurring in 33 of 4,962 diagnosis-record evaluations (0.67%). Nevertheless, automated error occurred in 9 of 33 disputed labels (27.3%; 95% CI: 12.1%-42.9%) compared with 53 of 4,929 consensus labels (1.08%; 95% CI: 0.79%-1.38%), an unadjusted risk ratio of 25.4. After adjustment for diagnosis and accounting for repeated evaluations within ECGs, disagreement remained associated with 22.8-fold higher risk of automated error (adjusted risk ratio: 22.8; 95% CI: 12.3-42.5; P<0.001) (Figure 1B). Viewed from the composition of the error pool, disputed labels represented 0.67% of evaluated labels but 9 of 62 model errors (14.5%), a 22-fold overrepresentation. These measures describe one association from complementary directions.

This should not be interpreted as evidence that the adjudicated reference was incorrect. Disagreement identifies diagnosis-record pairs for which assigning the reference diagnosis was difficult even for independent cardiologists. Once adjudication reduces such interpretations to a single binary label, that history of diagnostic difficulty is lost, and conventional evaluation treats an error on a unanimously interpreted label identically to an error on a diagnosis that required adjudication. Readjudication of discordant pediatric ECGs has similarly shown only modest agreement among expert electrophysiologists, supporting the view that apparent model errors may occur near genuine interpretive boundaries.(4) Persistence of the association after adjustment for diagnosis argues against its being explained solely by disagreement clustering within intrinsically difficult diagnostic classes. This distinction matters because most datasets preserve only the adjudicated endpoint, not information on how readily it was established; prior work has likewise emphasized comparison of model and expert agreement when assessing automated interpretation.(5)

Several limitations warrant caution. Only 33 disputed labels, including 9 disputed-label errors, were available, limiting precision and precluding diagnosis-specific inference. Consensus labels are by definition those on which both primary readers converged, so the comparison selects a more readily interpretable reference stratum and should be read as association rather than causation. Adjudication was performed with knowledge of the original interpretations. Public 12-lead ECG datasets preserving independent reader annotations remain uncommon, limiting external validation. These findings are therefore hypothesis-generating. Nonetheless, the observation that under 1% of evaluated labels accounted for approximately 15% of model errors suggests that pre-adjudication disagreement remains informative after a final diagnosis has been assigned. Preserving reader-level annotations and adjudication history may provide important context for future evaluation of automated ECG systems.
